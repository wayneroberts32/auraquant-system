Part 1: Global Principles & International Standards
These are overarching concepts that most regulatory bodies around the world adhere to.

Market Abuse & Manipulation:

Insider Trading: Trading based on material, non-public information is illegal everywhere. Your system must not use or act on such information.

Spoofing & Layering: Placing orders with the intent to cancel them before execution to create a false impression of supply or demand. This is a key algorithmic trading risk.

Pump and Dump: Artificially inflating the price of an asset (e.g., with a flurry of buy orders) to sell at a higher price.

Wash Trading: Simultaneously buying and selling the same asset to create misleading, artificial volume.

Know Your Customer (KYC) & Anti-Money Laundering (AML):

Your system/broker must verify the identity of its users.

It must monitor for and report suspicious transactions that could be related to money laundering or terrorist financing.

Best Execution:

A legal obligation to seek the best possible result for the client (or for yourself) when executing orders, considering price, costs, speed, and likelihood of execution.

Transparency & Disclosure:

If managing client funds, you must be transparent about your strategy, fees, and risks.

Part 2: Major Jurisdictions and Their Key Regulators
Here are some of the most influential financial markets and their regulatory bodies. The rules can differ significantly between them.

1. United States
Regulators:

Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC): Regulates securities (stocks, bonds).

Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC): Regulates derivatives (futures, options, forex).

Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA): A self-regulatory organization for broker-dealers.

Key Rules for Automated Systems:

Regulation Systems Compliance and Integrity (Reg SCI): For larger entities, requires robust technology controls, business continuity plans, and reporting of system issues.

Prohibition of Manipulative Trading Practices: Explicit rules against spoofing and layering (as part of the Dodd-Frank Act).

Pattern Day Trader (PDT) Rule: A retail trader with less than $25,000 in their account is limited in the number of day trades they can make in a 5-day period.

2. European Union / United Kingdom
Regulators:

European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA): EU-wide regulator.

Financial Conduct Authority (FCA): The UK regulator (post-Brexit).

National Regulators: e.g., BaFin (Germany), AMF (France).

Key Rules for Automated Systems:

Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID II): A huge piece of legislation. Key points:

Algorithmic Trading Identification: Firms engaged in algorithmic trading must be identified to regulators.

Systems and Controls: Must have effective systems and risk controls (e.g., kill switches) to prevent erroneous orders and system disruptions.

Tick-to-Trade Latency: Rules around the time it takes to process a market data update and send an order.

Transaction Reporting: Extensive reporting of all trades to regulators.

3. Japan
Regulator:

Financial Services Agency (FSA)

Key Rules:

Has strict regulations on algorithmic trading, including pre-approval of algorithms for certain participants.

Strong focus on market stability and preventing flash crashes.

4. Singapore
Regulator:

Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS)

Key Rules:

Securities and Futures Act (SFA): Governs market activities.

MAS has guidelines on risk management for automated trading, including testing, monitoring, and circuit breakers.

5. Australia
Regulator:

Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC)

Key Rules:

Market Integrity Rules: Specifically have rules for automated order processing, including testing requirements and controls to manage market impact.

Part 3: Broker & Exchange-Specific Rules
Your system must also comply with the rules of the broker and exchange you are connected to.

API Terms of Service: The agreement you sign with your broker (e.g., Interactive Brokers, OANDA) will have strict rules on how you can use their API.

Rate Limiting: Exchanges and brokers impose strict limits on the number of orders/cancellations per second. Exceeding these will result in disconnection or bans.

Short Selling Restrictions: Some markets have "uptick rules" or outright bans on short selling during volatile periods.

Exchange Mandated Circuit Breakers: Most exchanges have mechanisms that halt all trading if prices move too far, too fast. Your system must be able to handle these halts gracefully.

Part 4: How to Build This Into Your System's "Brain" (A Compliance Framework)
You cannot hardcode every rule from every country. Instead, build a framework that ensures compliance.

Pre-Trade Risk Checks (The Most Important Layer):

Maximum Order Size: Reject any single order above a set value/volume.

Maximum Position Size: Prevent the system from holding more than a predefined number of shares/contracts.

Maximum Order Rate: Enforce a limit on orders per second/minute to comply with broker/exchange limits.

Price Reasonability Checks: Reject orders that are too far from the current market price (e.g., a market buy order when the spread is abnormally wide).

Self-Trade Prevention: Ensure your system's orders cannot fill against each other.

Behavioral Logic (Preventing Market Abuse):

Order-to-Trade Ratio Monitor: Track the ratio of orders sent vs. orders filled. A very high ratio (e.g., 100:1) is a red flag for spoofing.

"Fill-or-Kill" and "Immediate-or-Cancel" Logic: Use these order types carefully to avoid being flagged for quote stuffing.

Kill Switch: Implement an immediate, hard-coded "kill switch" that stops all trading instantly. This is often a regulatory requirement.

Record Keeping & Logging:

Log every single action: every market data tick received, every order sent, every fill received, every cancellation.

Store these logs securely. Regulators can ask for this data for years after a trade.

Governance and Documentation:

Document Your Strategy: Clearly document how your algorithm works. This is needed for regulatory approval in many jurisdictions.

Change Control: Have a formal process for making changes to the algorithm's code and logic.

Backtesting & Simulation: Thoroughly test your strategy in a simulated environment to ensure it doesn't exhibit manipulative behavior under various market conditions.

Summary Checklist for Your System's Brain:
Risk Limits: Max order size, max position size, max loss limits.

Rate Limits: Orders/second caps to comply with broker rules.

Price Checks: Logic to avoid erroneous trades.

Order-to-Trade Monitoring: To prevent spoofing-like behavior.

Kill Switch: Instant, reliable shutdown.

Comprehensive Logging: Every action, timestamped and stored.

Legal Consultation: Have a professional review your setup for the specific markets you target.

Final and Most Critical Advice: Start by choosing one primary jurisdiction and one broker. Deeply research their specific, current rules. Do not try to build a system that is globally compliant on day one. The regulatory landscape is a minefield, and navigating it requires expert guidance.
